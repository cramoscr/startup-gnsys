# Product Mindset — Our Compass

This document is inspired by the **3Pillar Global Product Mindset** and is deliberately kept short.

It is not intended to become another heavy methodology. It is our compass when product development takes us far enough from shore that implementation pressure, attractive features, technical enthusiasm, or uncertainty can make us lose direction.

## What makes a digital product different

Three characteristics from 3Pillar's Product Mindset help us recognize the kind of product we are trying to build:

### 1. The product must be self-funded

A product must create enough economic value to justify and sustain its continued existence. Technical usefulness alone is not enough.

### 2. The product must be chosen

Users must choose the product because it creates value for them. Adoption cannot depend only on obligation, organizational mandate, or the fact that the software exists.

### 3. The product is never done

A successful digital product continually evolves as customer needs, markets, technology, and what we learn change. There is no meaningful final state in which product learning stops.

These characteristics give us three recurring tests:

- **Does it sustain itself economically?**
- **Do people choose it?**
- **Are we continuing to learn and evolve it?**

---

## Core Product Mindset Principles

### 1. Minimize Time to Value

Deliver useful value as early as possible. Do not wait for the entire envisioned system to exist before a real user can benefit.

### 2. Build to Actual Needs

Start with real customer needs and evidence. Features, technologies, interfaces, and implementation choices come afterward.

### 3. Excel at Change

Assume that learning will change the product. Favor decisions, architecture, and ways of working that allow us to adapt rather than defend yesterday's assumptions.

## Additional lens we retain — Build for Outcomes

Success is not measured by features completed or code produced. It is measured by meaningful outcomes for users and the business.

We retain this explicitly because it is a useful guardrail against becoming a feature factory.

---

## How we use this

When we are unsure what to build next, these questions bring us back on course:

1. **What real need are we solving?**
2. **What value can reach the user sooner?**
3. **What have we learned that should change our direction?**
4. **What outcome are we actually trying to produce?**
5. **Will users choose this?**
6. **Can the product economically sustain continued investment?**

> This is a compass, not a map.
