# Product Mindset — Reference Compass

This note keeps a lightweight comparison of several influential product-development perspectives. It is **reference material**, not an additional methodology.

Our primary compass remains the four principles recorded in [`product-mindset.md`](product-mindset.md), inspired by the 3Pillar Global Product Mindset.

## Comparison

| Approach | Main obsession | Short form |
|---|---|---|
| **3Pillar Product Mindset** | Value + adaptation + outcomes + need | Need → Value → Learn → Adapt |
| **Google / Design Sprint** | Reduce uncertainty quickly with users | Understand → Define → Sketch → Decide → Prototype → Validate |
| **Apple Design Principles** | Useful, simple, carefully crafted products | Purpose → Simplicity → Craft → Delight |
| **Y Combinator** | Build something people actually want | Launch → Talk to users → Iterate → Love → Grow |

## Google — Test before building too much

Google's Design Sprint approach emphasizes rapidly understanding a problem, defining it, exploring alternatives, choosing a direction, prototyping, and validating with users.

A particularly useful idea for us is that a prototype can simulate intelligence or automation before the underlying technology is fully built. This lets us test whether the interaction creates value before paying the full implementation cost.

**Reference:** https://design.google/library/design-sprints

**Related reference — simulating intelligence:** https://design.google/library/simulating-intelligence

## Apple — Simplicity and delight matter

Apple's public design principles emphasize purpose, agency, familiarity, flexibility, simplicity, craft, and delight, among other considerations.

A useful distinction for us is that **simplicity is not merely minimalism**. The goal is to remove unnecessary complexity while preserving what the user needs.

This resonates strongly with our own product principle:

> **Complejidad adentro → simplicidad afuera.**

Apple's emphasis on **delight** also matters. A product should not merely complete a transaction; the quality of the experience and how the user feels while accomplishing the task are legitimate product concerns.

**Reference:** https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/design-principles

## Y Combinator — Build something people want

YC's early-startup advice is deliberately pragmatic: launch, talk to users, learn from their behavior and feedback, iterate, and resist premature scaling.

The central reminder for us is:

> **Build something people want.**

Early evidence from a small number of customers who genuinely value the product is more important than appearing large before product-market fit exists.

**Reference:** https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/ycs-essential-startup-advice/

**Related reference — before you grow:** https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/before-you-grow/

---

## Our conclusion

We do **not** need to combine these approaches into a larger framework.

The four 3Pillar-inspired principles remain our working compass:

1. **Minimize Time to Value**
2. **Excel at Change**
3. **Build for Outcomes**
4. **Solve for Need**

The other perspectives provide two particularly useful checks:

- **YC:** *Do people actually want it?*
- **Apple:** *Do people enjoy using it?*

Google provides a practical experimental discipline: **prototype and expose assumptions to users before committing to expensive implementation.**

> The references help us check whether our compass still points north; they do not replace the compass.
